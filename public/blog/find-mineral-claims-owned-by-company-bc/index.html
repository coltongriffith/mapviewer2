How to Find Mineral Claims Owned by a Company in British Columbia

To find mineral claims held by a company in British Columbia, search the province's published tenure data by registered owner. In Exploration Maps, open Add Claims → Search Claims Registry, select British Columbia, choose the registered-owner mode and enter the company name. Matches are returned as candidates grouped by confidence — company names reach the registry in clipped legal form and under previous names — so you confirm which titles are actually theirs before mapping them. Authoritative ownership must be confirmed in Mineral Titles Online.

The search, step by step

Open Exploration Maps and choose Add Claims → Search Claims Registry. Select British Columbia, choose the registered-owner mode, and type the company name. Start with the distinctive part of the name rather than the full legal form — 'Goliath' will find more than 'Goliath Resources Limited' if the registry recorded it differently.

Read the candidates properly

Results come back grouped: exact name match, possible match, and weak match. That grouping is deliberate and it is worth respecting.

Company names reach a title registry in clipped legal form, with inconsistent punctuation, under previous corporate names, through numbered subsidiaries, and in the name of one joint-venture partner rather than the operator everyone talks about. A name match ranks a candidate; it does not establish who holds the ground. Nothing enters your map until you tick it.

What a name match is and is not
Owner search matches the registered-owner field in the province's published tenure data. It is a search aid. It is not a determination of legal ownership, and a company's real land position may include titles held under a name you did not search. Confirm ownership in Mineral Titles Online.

Select the whole position at once

Each candidate group has a select-all control, and the footer totals what you have selected — claim count and hectares together. Those two numbers are the land position, and they are the numbers you will end up quoting, so it is worth reading them before you add rather than counting rows afterwards.

Hectares in particular tends to surprise people: forty small cell claims and four large leases can describe very different companies.

Map it

Add the selected tenures and the map fits to their extent. Assign the Claims role for standard styling, and give the layer a name that will read in the legend — 'Peer Ground' or 'Optioned Claims' rather than the default. If you are comparing two companies, add each as its own layer with different stroke colours; they become separate legend entries automatically.

Watch ground you don't own

A monitored group of claims is a watch list, not an ownership record. You can add a competitor's titles to it and get told when the published record for them changes — a good-to-date that moved, an owner added or removed, a title that stopped appearing in the dataset.

That is often more useful than the initial search. A neighbour's claim reaching its good-to-date, or changing hands, is exactly the kind of event you would rather learn about early. Nothing about monitoring is visible to the title holder, and nothing about it changes the title.

A change is not an opportunity until you have checked
A status change, a termination date, or a title dropping out of the published dataset is what the published data shows. It is not confirmation that ground is available. Records leave a published dataset for several reasons, including partial publication at the province. Check the tenure in MTO before acting on it.

Where this workflow stops

For a transaction, use it to build the picture and then verify every tenure in MTO.

  • It reflects published data as of the last successful synchronization, not the live registry
  • It matches a registered-owner field, so beneficial ownership and option agreements are invisible to it
  • It cannot tell you about applications in progress beyond what the province publishes
  • It is not a substitute for a title search when money is changing hands

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I search BC mineral claims by company name?
Yes. Exploration Maps searches the province's published tenure data by registered owner and returns candidates grouped by match confidence. Company names are often recorded in clipped legal form, so try the distinctive part of the name on its own. Confirm ownership in Mineral Titles Online before relying on it.
How do I find out who owns a mineral claim in BC?
Look up the tenure number — in Exploration Maps for the published registered owner and a map of the boundary, or in Mineral Titles Online for the authoritative record. For anything that governs a transaction, MTO is the answer that counts.
Can I see a competitor's whole land position?
You can see the titles published under a registered-owner name matching your search, with a claim count and total hectares. That is usually most of it, but it will miss ground held under a subsidiary, a previous corporate name, or a joint-venture partner's name — so treat the result as a strong starting point rather than a complete answer.
Is it legal to look up another company's mineral claims?
Yes. BC mineral tenure information is public data published by the province under the Open Government Licence – British Columbia. Searching, mapping and monitoring it is ordinary competitive research, and nothing about doing so is visible to the title holder.
Can I monitor claims that belong to someone else?
Yes. A monitored group is a watch list rather than an ownership record, so a competitor's ground, a property you are evaluating, or a block next to your own can all be added and tracked with the same reminders as your own claims.
